Why Do Timezones Exist?

Before the 19th century, every town set its own clock by the sun. Noon was the moment the sun sat at its highest point over that town — and since the sun cannot be overhead in two places at once, no two towns agreed. It did not matter, because nothing moved fast enough for the disagreement to be noticeable. Then railways happened.

Suddenly a schedule had to be readable in two towns at once. Bristol solar time was about ten minutes behind London, so railway timetables had to reconcile clocks that disagreed.

Railways and governments adopted standard time during the 19th century. In 1884, delegates from 25 nations met in Washington D.C. and adopted the Greenwich meridian as the prime meridian and a universal day. The familiar 24 one-hour bands are a useful geometric model, not the boundaries imposed by that conference: governments choose real zones, offsets, and exceptions.

Three names for one instant. A timetable cannot be written.

STANDARD TIME one shared zone (GMT)
@for (town of standardisedTowns; track town) {
{{ town }} 12:00
}

One name for one instant. The cost: the sun is no longer exactly overhead at noon.

That trade is the whole story. A timezone deliberately sacrifices astronomical accuracy to buy a name that many people can agree on. Every bug you will ever write about dates is a bug about which of those two things you were actually holding.

The Core Concept

🕐

UTC is the Anchor

UTC is the international time standard and the zero-offset reference. APIs also accept UTC as a zone identifier. It never shifts for daylight saving.

🔢

Offset = Difference

An offset says how much to add to UTC to get local wall-clock time. New York is -05:00 in winter and -04:00 in summer. An offset is a snapshot, not an identity.

🏷️

IANA Names

Because offsets change, we identify zones by region: America/New_York. That name selects a set of civil-time rules from the runtime's copy of the IANA database. Historical coverage and future rules depend on the database release, so keep runtimes current.

Key Takeaways

  • → A JavaScript Date is an object whose time value is a timestamp. It does not store an IANA timezone; the runtime applies the host's zone when it renders local text.
  • → When you display a Date, the runtime applies a zone to format it. The underlying timestamp never changes.
  • → Timezone bugs live at the boundary: the moment you convert between a timestamp and a human-readable string, in either direction.
  • → "An instant" and "a wall-clock reading" are different types. Most date bugs are one being silently used where the other was meant.
